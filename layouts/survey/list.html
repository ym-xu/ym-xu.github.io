{{/* ---------- 6 · method ---------- */}}

Method

Every morning (06:00 UTC) a GitHub Action asks the arXiv API for the last {{ $d.weeks }} weeks of papers in cs.CV, cs.CL, cs.IR and neighbouring categories that mention documents, OCR, layout, tables, charts, forms and the like. Only first releases count: a new version or cross-listing of an older paper is skipped.

A paper has to be about documents — document terms are scored (title three times, abstract once) and must reach a threshold — and must look visual, so text-only “document” work is dropped; a list of negative patterns catches the usual false friends (pathology “slides”, nautical “charts”, robots, sensors). Each of the {{ len $d.subfields }} subfields is a keyword rule scored the same way; the strongest one is the paper’s main subject, with the catch-all “Document QA & VLMs” discounted so specific subfields win. Retrieval, agent, long-document and extraction papers also need a strong visual cue, and agent papers a stronger document signal. Papers that apply OCR or an LLM to a domain task (exam grading, clinical or legal documents, signature verification…) are kept in the list as “applications” but left out of every figure. Each subfield’s “recent” papers are its own main-subject papers from the last eight weeks, ranked by venue acceptance, citations and public code; a hand-picked entry replaces them.

Semantic Scholar adds author IDs (so namesakes stay apart), citation counts and venues. On top of the rules sits a hand-kept file, data/survey_curated.toml: papers to exclude or force-include, representative papers per subfield, and my picks. The rules themselves live in scripts/fetch_survey.py. It remains a rough instrument — a map of where attention goes, not a curated reading list.1
